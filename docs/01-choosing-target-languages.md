# Chapter 1 — Choosing Target Languages

**The one-sentence version:** don't pick the biggest market; pick the market where *your* book solves *their* problem, where your niche is underserved, and where you can afford a real human reviewer.

## 1.1 Why "biggest language" is the wrong default

It's tempting to rank languages by population or by Kindle store size: Spanish! Mandarin! Hindi! That ranking answers a question nobody asked. The question that matters is:

> *For the readers I want, in this language, is my book — translated carefully and priced fairly — obviously better than what they can already buy today?*

Three factors decide that answer, and market size is only one of them:

1. **Demand for your niche.** A Spanish-language self-published author writing "ADHD-friendly budgeting for freelancers" faces different competition than someone writing epic fantasy.
2. **Supply and quality of existing competition.** If every existing book in the niche is either translated from English (poorly) or thin, you have room to be the *good* one.
3. **Your ability to reach readers there.** Can you run ads? Find communities? Get the book into the stores they shop? A readership you can't reach is not a readership.

## 1.2 The shortlist method

Don't research thirty languages. In one sitting, write down **five candidate languages**. You already have signals without any research:

- **Reader emails and reviews.** Any non-English reviews on your book? Any reader messages in another language? Those people are early adopters for that edition.
- **Your book's natural geography.** A book about FFXIV has an obvious Japanese community. A book about reselling finds rabid Portuguese and Spanish-speaking reseller communities. A nursing-scenario AI guide has Spanish and Filipino English (skip the latter) but genuinely big German and Japanese healthcare-professional markets.
- **Print markets that still respect self-publishing.** Germany, Japan, and Italy have cultures of buying printed books; the US market share of print is unusual. A niche book can live longer on a German bookshelf than in Kindle Unlimited.
- **Where your free content is read.** If you have a blog, YouTube channel, or GitHub project, look at your audience's language distribution. Readers who already found you in English are warm leads for the translated edition.

From the five candidates, cut to **two or three semifinalists** using the scoring sheet below.

## 1.3 The scoring sheet (30 minutes, all novelists get the same sheet)

Score each candidate language 1–5 on six factors. It forces you to separate *this market is big* from *this market is big **for me***.

| Factor | Score 1 means | Score 5 means |
|---|---|---|
| **Niche demand** | Little evidence anyone there wants this topic | Active communities, bestsellers, or search interest in this exact niche |
| **Underserved niche** | Many current books cover it well | Existing offerings are gaps, bad translations, or nothing |
| **Reachability** | No ads, no communities, no free-content audience there | You can name three concrete ways readers there will hear about it |
| **Your cost to translate + review** | Reviewers for this language are rare and expensive for this genre | You can identify a plausible reviewer and rough rate today |
| **Reader willingness to buy** | Market is dominated by free reading apps / piracy is the norm there | Readers demonstrably buy indie-translated books in your price range |
| **Second-book potential** | One book is the ceiling for you in this language | A catalog could grow here (series, adjacent topics, same identity/pen name) |

Add the scores. A language scoring **25+** is a strong candidate. A language scoring below **18** usually means *one strong factor is papering over five weak ones* — often enthusiasm without reachability, or size without demand for your niche. Physical size of the market doesn't outrank the six factors; it's just one input to one factor (niche demand).

## 1.4 Why the reviewer matters at selection time — not budget time

Here's a mistake that quietly kills projects: an author picks a language for the market, then discovers nobody qualified will review the translation at their budget. The reviewer is the single most important hire in the whole process (Chapter [03](03-budgeting-ai-plus-human-review.md)). So the selection question is also: **"Do I know someone — or have a credible path to someone — who both reads and writes this language fluently, understands my genre, and will review AI output?"**

Practical moves:

- Look at translators' author pages on platforms like Upwork, Fiverr Pro, ProZ, Babelcube, or translators' associations (e.g., ATA in the US, ITI in the UK, BDÜ in Germany). Check for: genre match, published translation credits, and (for Babelcube-style platforms) willingness to work on revenue-share.
- If you have any referral path — a bilingual reader, a friend of a friend, someone in your newsletter abroad — use it. Referrals routinely beat cold outreach on quality.
- If you can't find a single candidate reviewer for a language, drop that language regardless of its market score. It will still be there in a year, when your QA skills and catalog are stronger.

## 1.5 The three starter languages worth considering seriously

If you want a default starting set to evaluate against the scoring sheet rather than starting from a blank page, these three come up again and again in independent-author translation data:

### Spanish (es)
- **Reach:** Huge Kindle and print markets in Spain, Mexico, Argentina, Colombia, and the US. Amazon, Kobo, and Google Play all have meaningful Spanish-language stores.
- **Book type that works here:** Broad nonfiction how-to, business books, self-help, and — for fiction — romance and crime, not everything.
- **Watch out:** Spain and Latin America are different markets stylistically. Decide whether you'll target *neutral Latin American Spanish* (widely understood, common for books intended for the whole region) or *Spain-Castilian*. Ask your reviewer which variety they themselves represent — this is a real question with real consequences.

### Portuguese (pt)
- **Brazil specifically.** Amazon.com.br exists, Kobo and Apple Books are active, and Brazil's readership of translated how-to and self-help is strong. Also, Kindle Unlimited-style models have a beachhead there.
- **Watch out:** European Portuguese readers often bristle at Brazilian-idiom text. If you target Brazil, say so on your cover region and metadata. Cost of Brazilian reviewers is typically lower than, say, Japanese or German reviewers, which makes Brazil an attractive *second* language after a first success.
- **Data note:** Independent-author communities consistently report Brazil as one of the better non-English markets for translated indies, and often *the* best market for translated romance and self-help.

### Chinese (zh), Japanese (ja), and other "second-rank" candidates
- Chinese: enormous native readership; Kindle China closed in 2022, leaving WeChat-based and other app stores that have their own rules. Non-trivial for a solo author. Score it honestly on reachability.
- Japanese: huge book-buying culture and strong niche demand (business, how-to, chess, gaming guides) but also one of the most demanding grammar/style gaps from English, which shows in reviewer costs.
- Italian and French: real markets, often at reasonable reviewer costs, good for how-to and self-help. Common third languages for successful translators — worth scoring if your first edition works.
- German: Europe's largest book market in many niche, tech, and business categories; candidates on the costlier end but with a strong culture of paying full price for books.

None of these are recommendations for *your* book. They are the languages to hold up against the scoring sheet so you're not choosing based on vibes.

## 1.6 One candidate worth scoring separately: Amazon's own translation tools

Amazon Publishing offers a service (Kindle Direct Publishing's translation program, when open — it has opened and closed by invitation over the years) that will machine-translate, edit, and publish your book into select languages with them handling p&l. When open, it uses Amazon's own AI translation tools and human editors, charges you nothing upfront, and takes a revenue share. Key things to check if you encounter it:

- **Subscribe to updates when it's closed.** Invitations have historically opened and closed with Amazon's own internal capacity, so being on the right mailing list matters.
- **Check the rights grant.** Historically, invitations included a *license composited* — you grant Amazon rights to the translation they produce, typically with limited or no ability to take that translation elsewhere. Chapter [02](02-rights-in-plain-english.md) covers why "free" offers with binding grants deserve a slow read.
- **Check the language catalog.** The set of languages Amazon offers has varied (Spanish, German, Italian, French, and others depending on the program phase). If yours isn't on the list, it's not an option for you anyway.
- **Consider it as a channel, not a strategy.** A free, Amazon-only translated edition can be a great test of demand before you commit to your own multi-store edition with full rights.

## 1.7 Timing your decision

A few timing heuristics that come from watching both sides of this:

- **Don't translate book #1 of a series until books #2 and #3 exist in English.** Translating a series opener and leaving readers hanging is the most common series-translation failure.
- **Do translate when your English edition is stable.** If you're still fixing typos in the English source, fixing them in three languages is 3x the work. Freeze the source first (Chapter [04](04-the-workflow-draft-to-final.md)).
- **Do translate in the same season your genre peaks.** Budgeting guides sell in January; a romance set at Christmas should launch well before Christmas — anywhere in the world. Calendars as marketing tools work across borders better than we pretend.
- **Allow 6–10 weeks** from "clean source file ready" to "book live" as a first-timer, and add a week per language beyond the first. Chapter [04](04-the-workflow-draft-to-final.md) breaks down that timeline.

## Bottom line

Choosing the language is a research decision, not an enthusiasm decision. Score at least two languages you *like* and one you *don't* find exciting, so the exercise can't rationalize a favorite. Let the six factors — niche demand, underserved-ness, reachability, reviewer availability, reader behavior, and second-book potential — tell you which language has the weakest link. Pick the language where you can name, today, a likely reviewer and three concrete ways readers there will hear about your book. If you can't, keep scoring.
