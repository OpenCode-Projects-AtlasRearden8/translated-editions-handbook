# Selling Translated Editions: A Handbook for Authors

*How to take your book into other languages — choosing where to go, paying for AI-assisted translation with real human review, checking the result like a pro, protecting your rights, and finding readers who don't speak your language.*

By **Dylan Asdale** · Published by **Asdale Dynamics, LLC**

---

## Who this handbook is for

You already have a book. Maybe it's doing well in English, maybe it's just finding its feet. Either way, you've noticed something: not everyone who might love your book reads English. A large share of the world's readers live in languages like Spanish, Portuguese, German, Japanese, Italian, French, and Hindi — and most of the books they can buy are not yours, yet.

This handbook walks you through selling **translated editions** of your book, step by step, in plain English. No jargon walls, no gatekeeping. If a term matters, we explain it the first time we use it and then keep using it.

We are going to be honest with you, too. Translating a book is one of the higher-effort, higher-reward moves available to an independent author. It works when you treat the translation as a **real edition of a real book** — which is exactly how readers abroad will treat it. It fails when it's rushed, unreviewed, or machine output pasted into a template. This handbook shows you how to do it the first way, with AI as a tool that makes the work affordable rather than as a shortcut that skips the work.

## What you should know going in

**1. You are the publisher now.** When you translate your own book, you pick the language, the freelancer, the tools, the QA standard, the price, and the publication date. That is freedom, and it is also homework. This handbook gives you the homework answers so nothing surprises you later.

**2. AI changes the cost math — it does not change the care required.** A few years ago, translating a 60,000-word book professionally cost five figures, so most independent authors simply couldn't. AI-assisted translation plus a human reviewer brings the total down to a fraction of that — often to a few hundred dollars per language for careful work. The steps that were always non-negotiable (human review, QA, a native speaker who can read the joke) are still non-negotiable. You're just finally able to afford them.

**3. Machine-only translation will hurt you.** There is a well-documented pattern: a machine-translated book goes up, readers in that language leave reviews saying it reads like a robot, and the author's name in that market is quietly done. Reader reviews in non-English stores are often blunter than English ones. Treat one careful human pass as cheap insurance on your own name.

**4. Rights come first, always.** Before you spend any money, you need a clean answer to "Do I have the legal right to publish a translation of this book?" Chapter [02](02-rights-in-plain-english.md) sorts that out in plain English for the most common situations.

## Three myths worth killing now

- **"Translation is just swapping words."** It's transferring meaning, tone, and jokes across cultures. A great translation sounds like it was *written* in the target language. That's your quality bar.
- **"If I machine-translate it, I'll just sell a few copies anyway."** Bad translations don't just fail to sell — they generate public evidence (reviews, reader complaints, refund spikes) that follows your brand in that language permanently.
- **"The biggest languages are automatically the best markets."** Market size matters less than *competition within your niche* and *how discoverable your book is*. A mid-size language where nobody covers your niche can beat a giant language where fifty authors already do. Chapter [01](01-choosing-target-languages.md) gives you a scoring method.

## How to use this handbook

Read chapters 01, 02, and 03 in order — they answer the three questions that come before any money moves: *where, may I, and how much*. Chapters 04–08 are the working phase. Chapter 09 is a set of templates you will actually open and copy from, including a one-page budget worksheet and a rights-note format you can adapt.

Every chapter ends with a short **Bottom line** summary. If you only read the bottom lines, you'll still make far better decisions than most authors entering translation — but the real advantage is in the details, and the details are written to be readable.

## A note on scope

This handbook is about **translated editions of books** — choosing languages, budgeting translation, QA, layout, rights, and marketing to readers abroad. It deliberately stays out of two neighbor topics covered elsewhere:

- **Personal, everyday AI translation** (using translation apps for travel, chat, and daily life) — that's a different book.
- **Distribution to non-Amazon stores in general** (how wide channels like Draft2Digital and PublishDrive work, store by store) — also its own manual. Here, we cover stores only as they intersect with translation, like Amazon's own translation marketplace and metadata in other languages.

One more scope note, because this industry needs it said plainly: nothing in this handbook is legal advice. Rights and contracts are exactly the area where "plain English explanation" and "legal counsel" differ. We tell you what questions to ask and what answers to be suspicious of; a lawyer (or your publisher's legal team) answers the ones with money on the table.
