# Chapter 7 — Marketing to Readers Abroad

**The one-sentence version:** readers in another country will not find out about your book by accident — you have to show up where they already look for books, in their language, with a message written for them.

Your translated edition is live on the store. Now comes the part that decides whether it sells five copies to people searching your exact keywords, or five hundred copies across a real readership. This chapter covers what works, what doesn't, and what to do first.

## 7.1 The mindset shift: you're a foreign author now

In English, you have years of accumulated credibility: a covers-blurbs rhythm you know, review expectations you understand, niche communities you can navigate. In the new market, you are starting from zero *on purpose*, but with one unfair advantage: **a book that already exists and a track record you can point to.**

Three things change when you market abroad:

1. **The communities are different.** Reddit is dominant in US book discovery, but Spanish-language book talk happens on Goodreads-es, Instagram, BookTok-es and Telegram channels; German book culture is strongly shaped by blogs, lovelybooks.de, IG, and print-media reviews; Brazilian readers congregate on Skoob, Instagram, TikTok, and YouTube; French-life revolves around Babelio and culture podcasts.
2. **The content formats differ.** Text-length norms, humor, and even post frequency vary. Spanish and Brazilian social media is warmer and more personal; German book talk is more informational; Japanese online book marketing is very visual and brief.
3. **Your competitivity level is different.** If you were a known name in your English niche, you're an unknown in the new market. Budget your expectations: first-year translated sales of 100–500 copies per language, well-marketed with good reviews, is a **normal, respectable result** — and it paths to more as you become known.

## 7.2 What actually drives translated-book discovery on stores

Before social marketing, remember that stores themselves are the biggest channel for translated indies. Three levers, in order of ROI:

### 7.2.1 Keywords and metadata (the 30-minute process, per language)

Store search is the #1 way foreign readers encounter a new translated book. Steps:

1. **Generate candidate keywords in the target language.** Use AI: "give me 25 search phrases a reader in [country] might type when looking for a book about [your topic], in [language], including long-tail and colloquial ones." Review with your translator for each phrase's naturalness.
2. **Verify against the store.** Type each phrase into the Amazon.es / .de / .com.br search bar with your browser set as if you were in that country (or use the store's own search with your location; Amazon's search preview responds to your account's country settings). What comes up? If a keyword returns junk or nothing, don't use it.
3. **Check the competition.** For each candidate keyword, look at the first 10 results: are they in-language, self-published, well-reviewed? If the results are all big-house books or untranslated English titles, you may have found an open keyword — gold.
4. **Assign keywords.** Amazon gives 7 keyword slots (per market); choose phrases, not single words. Categories: check the target-language category structure at upload time, not from memory.

Time budget on first language: 1–2 hours. On the second: 30 minutes.

### 7.2.2 Reviews, plainly gotten

Foreign-language reviews are the trust layer in the new market and the single biggest barrier between browsers and buyers. Honest ways to get them going:

- **Your existing readers.** Some of your English readers fluently read both languages. Invite them: "If you read Spanish and liked the English edition, honest early reviews of the Spanish one would help enormously." Never pay, never trade, never family-review. Just invite.
- **Your newsletter.** Non-English speakers exist in your English newsletter. A simple "do you read [language]?" email segment them, then invite readers to early review.
- **Advance reader copies (ARCs) in the language.** Send your reviewer or a handful of natives the final file with a review invitation. This is normal author behavior in all markets; frame it as a review copy, not a purchase.
- **Editorial Reviews.** Amazon's "editorial reviews" section in KDP lets you place quotes. A real quote from your reviewer (with their name and professional credentials — they're a professional in that market, and their assessment is a *credible* endorsement) works well in language.

### 7.2.3 The blurb (re-written, not translated — see Chapter [04](04-the-workflow-draft-to-final.md), step 6)

We said it in the workflow chapter and will repeat it once: **the blurb is the highest-conversion text on the page.** Pay your translator (or a copywriter they recommend) to *write a native-feeling* blurb instead of one translated from yours. Cost: $30–$80. ROI: everything even when everything else is free.

## 7.3 Store-side marketing levers that work for translations

| Lever | Effort | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **BookBub (international deals)** | Medium | BookBub has country/language-specific editions (bookbub.com/es, .de, and regional listings). A BookBub Featured Deal in a foreign language is rare-payment; the free "New Book" listing and profile are free — set those up. |
| **Amazon ads in the foreign store** | Medium | Amazon.es/.de/.com.br ads work the same as US ads; often lower competition and lower CPC (cost-per-click, i.e., what you pay per ad click). Start with a small daily budget ($3–5) on highly-verified keywords only. Rule: know which local keywords your language's buyers use first (7.2.1) before spending. |
| **Kindle Countdown / price promos** | Low | Set in KDP per store; price-promo calendars in foreign markets include local events (Back-to-School in Brazil is February, Spain's "El Día del Libro" is April 23, Germany's Buchmesse weeks) — one local-calendar anchor per quarter. |
| **KDP Select in foreign store** | Low | If the translated edition is enrolled in Select for that market, free-promo and countdown levers work in that language and often have less saturation. If your English edition is Select-enrolled, this doesn't conflict (see Chapter 2.2). |
| **A "translated edition" launch bundle (box-of-three pricing with EN)** | Low | Offer the EN + ES editions as two options on your author site. Slight discount for the pair. Drives cross-market adoption. |
| **Author-page localization** | Low | Amazon author central exists per market; set your bio in-language for .es/.de/.jp and keep books synced. Cost: an hour, and the bio translation may be your reviewer's 15-minute favor. |

## 7.4 Social and community marketing (pick two; skip the rest)

The biggest mistake authors make is trying to exist in every channel in a language they don't speak. Pick **two** channels where your audience demonstrably gathers and commit to them properly.

**Properly** means: posts written in the language (with your translator's help for the first batch, then a template + AI rough draft that your translator spot-checks monthly), presence at least twice a week, and someone who actually answers replies in the language. If nobody on your team reads replies in that language, you need a plan for replies (a $20/month social-media-native freelancer checking comments twice a week, or an arrangement where your reviewer triages weekly).

Where translated-book authors find traction, by language/market and niche type:

- **Spanish:** BookTok-es and Instagram are strong for fiction; LinkedIn-es and X/Twitter-es for nonfiction and business; Facebook groups still matter enormously in Latin America, especially for budgeting, health-adjacent, and how-to nonfiction. Telegram channels for book deals have real reach.
- **Portuguese (Brazil):** Skoob (the Brazilian Goodreads) is the single most useful fiction-community channel; Instagram/TikTok is the main visual discovery for both fiction and nonfiction; YouTube is big for technical and self-improvement book content.
- **German:** lovelybooks.de is the reading community; Facebook groups skew to self-improvement hobbies; LinkedIn.de and Xing for business nonfiction. German book bloggers and podcasts still have meaningful power to move niche nonfiction — outreach to 5 bloggers/podcasters per book is realistic and cheap.
- **Japanese:** X (Twitter) is where Japanese book talk lives (book hashtags have extreme depth); note-pickup engines like note.com host long-form book-review essays; YouTube booktubers for how-to niches.
- **Italian/French:** Instagram + book blogs for both; Babelio (FR) is the Goodreads equivalent.

If your niche is *nonfiction how-to/education* (nursing+AI, reselling, budgeting, productivity), the highest-yield move in almost every market is **the same as English: appear in the niche's own spaces** — reseller communities, nurse communities, ADHD communities. Those communities exist in your target languages too, and a genuinely useful post from a real author travels better than any ad.

## 7.5 The translator as marketing asset

Your reviewer/translator is on the ground with both linguistic and cultural fluency for your market. Two non-Icky, fair-value asks:

1. **A native blurb + subtitle pass** (discussed above; paid as a small add-on).
2. **A "market notes" memo** ($50–$100 add-on): 1–2 pages on how this niche is marketed in their country, three communities worth knowing, three competitor books worth studying, and local calendar hooks. You'll reuse it for years if this becomes a real market for you.

Do ask your translator if they *also* do translation-adjacent work (some run book social channels; some have journalist contacts). Do not ask for their community work for free — expertise-in-a-language is just as scarce and valuable as the translation itself. Hourly market rates apply.

## 7.6 Your hub site abroad (beyond the stores)

If you have a website (Dylan Asdale's dylanasdale.github.io is the model for this — the hub site does the "explain like a friend" work in English), decide deliberately whether to run a translated landing page:

- **When it's worth it:** you're building long-term brand presence in a language (multiple books planned); you have a real hub, not just a store page; your audiences abroad can reach you by email.
- **When to skip it for now:** you sell one book, sales will be store-generated, and a stale half-translated website reads *worse* than no website. Get it right on the stores first; launch the hub page when the second edition goes live.

If you do a translated landing page: transcreate (rewrite for local readers) rather than machine-translate; include local context ("disponible en Amazon.es, Amazon.com.mx, y librerías locales" — "available at..." per store region); link the store pages for that country; and set a *realistic* review-maintenance cadence — a Spanish reader emailing you in Spanish deserves a reply in Spanish within a week, plan for how.

## 7.7 Pricing translated editions (the 3-ticket rule)

Price. Get this right; it's the free money on the table.

1. **Match the market's book-buying reality.** In the US, a $3.99–$5.99 ebook is normal; in Brazil and Mexico, price sensitivity is sharper and $2.99–$4.99 converts better; in Germany and Japan, full-price ebooks ($6–$12) sell fine because print prices anchor higher. Pick a price in the *new market's* normal band.
2. **Don't underprice to signal cheapness.** In German and Japanese markets, a too-cheap ebook reads as low-quality more often than it reads as a bargain. Price at the market, not below it.
3. **Account for the 60/35 split rule.** On Amazon, 70% royalty applies between certain min/max prices per store that differ by currency; a price that earns 70% in the US store might be under the minimum to reach 70% in Amazon.com.br or .mx. Check the 70% price band in each target store and confirm your price is within it before launch (or accept the 35% band knowingly).

Practical per-language reality check at launch: for the Spanish edition, check Amazon.es, Amazon.com.mx, and Amazon.com's Spanish store separately — different price bands, different expectations, same book.

## 7.8 Advertising comparison shopping: US vs. foreign stores

A rough picture of what ads look and cost like on the biggest stores for nonfiction how-to in 2026 (CPC is the auction price you pay per ad click; impressions are what the ad display counts):

| Store | Typical CPC for self-help/how-to | Saturation | Practical note |
|---|---|---|---|
| Amazon US (English) | $0.30–$0.70 | Very high | You probably know this market already |
| Amazon.es / Amazon.de / Amazon.it / Amazon.fr | $0.10–$0.35 | Low–medium | Your main "go run the ads" playbook from your book's English launch translates directly, at cheaper clicks |
| Amazon.com.br / .com.mx | $0.08–$0.25 | Low | Watch conversion; price bands matter more than CPC |
| Google Play Books (multi-language) | partner platform | Low | Its own question; worth a look once you have a stable catalog |

The low saturation of Amazon.de/it/fr/es book categories for many niches is a real, asymmetric advantage over English-market saturation. If you learn keyword bidding once, the skill transfers to cheaper inventory abroad.

## Bottom line

Abroad-marketing is: (1) three levers on the store itself — keywords researched in the new language, early reviews from real readers in that language, and a native-feeling blurb; (2) a small number of community channels you can actually sustain in that language; (3) prices set for the market, not converted from America. Run the store-side levers before you sink time into social media, and pick the social channels you can genuinely sustain. The first 100 reviews won't come fast; the first 10 usually will, and they'll tell you whether the market wants more of you.
