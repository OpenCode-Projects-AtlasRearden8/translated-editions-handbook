Going Live, Staying Live

Chapter 8

The one-sentence version: publish with a checklist, set a price per market, watch the first 48 hours like a nurse watching a chart, and then maintain the edition on a light yearly cadence so it never becomes a stale liability.

This is the practical chapter about the day you hit publish and the years after. Nothing here is clever; all of it is the kind of boring that prevents expensive.

8.1 Pre-publish checklist (run once, per edition)

Do this the day before you upload. It takes 90 minutes and it's skipped by the authors who later eat the cost.

The file itself

  • [ ] Final file is the reviewer-corrected + QA-fixed version, not the AI draft and not a pre-review version.
  • [ ] Title page and copyright page reflect the translated edition (translation credit line, translated publisher line, correct original-publication info).
  • [ ] TOC links all work in the actual reader app (click-test in the store preview and in the Kindle app download).
  • [ ] Code blocks, tables, and images render — spot-check 10 pages at random, including the densest chapter.
  • [ ] No translator notes blocks, no TODO strings, no placeholder text anywhere.

Assets and store listing

  • [ ] Cover file: translated text, thumbnail-legible, spine width matches final page count if print.
  • [ ] Blurb: native-feeling, written for the market, clean line-to-line reads with no English leftovers.
  • [ ] Subtitle: adapted (not literally translated), reads naturally as a marketing line.
  • [ ] Keywords: researched in-language (Chapter 07), verified in that store's search.
  • [ ] Categories: set in the target-language store's own structure.
  • [ ] Price: set for each market, inside that store's 70%-royalty price band (Chapter 07, 7.7).
  • [ ] Author bio (author central): translated for that market's author page.

Rights and records

  • [ ] Rights memo filled out (Chapter 02 template): what version, who reviewed, which stores, any limits.
  • [ ] Working files (source, AI draft, reviewed file, cover layered file, transcript of translator notes) saved in your 09-live/ folder or backed up.
  • [ ] Translator contract signed and filed; payment logged.
  • [ ] Store's current AI-disclosure policy re-read the week you publish — policies move, your memo shouldn't assume last year's wording.

8.2 Publish day

The sequence on upload day, for Amazon KDP (each store is similar; this is the pattern):

  1. Upload, save as draft, order a proof (print) or preview (ebook). For ebooks, use the online previewer and download the file and open in a real device's app. For print, order a physical proof copy — even one $8 copy catches layout bugs digital previews don't.
  2. Buy your own copy on the live store in your target market (switch your account's country or send yourself a gift). This verifies the real purchase flow, the real delivered file, and the real price a reader sees. It also lets you see whether Amazon's auto-preview "Look Inside" serves the right chapters.
  3. Set the launch date (or publish immediately if there's no reason to wait; pre-orders add risk in foreign markets you can't yet support).
  4. Announce where you actually have people. Your English newsletter (with translated-readers callout), your social channels per Chapter 7's pick-two. Do not spam communities you haven't participated in yet — post a value post first (the "here's how I translated my book with AI + human review, AMA" story post does very well in author/indie spaces, and it markets the book incidentally).
  5. Tell your translator. Send them the live link. They will proof the live listing with sharper eyes than the last review pass, free, because it now has their name's shadow on it.

8.3 The first 48 hours

A short intense watch, mostly to catch the same four failure modes across the process:

WindowWhat you're watching forIf you see it
First 6 hoursListing renders correctly in each country's store; price is what you set; sample/Look-Inside correctFix immediately — these affect every future sale
First 48 hoursFirst readers' reactions (no sales? no reviews?)?? if zero sales in 48 hours: see 8.4 below
First weekReviews (once they arrive) in-language; refund rate, if accessibleOne review calling it robotic/machine-y = QA bug, stop promo, investigate
First monthKeyword placement for your target phrases; category ranking; ad conversion if advertisingRanking placement = biggest market-signal lever still available

Don't panic in the first 48 hours if the book has no sales. Foreign-language catalogs take longer to find their first readers; the Kindle Store's foreign sections are less flooded but also have less browsing traffic. Give it two weeks before reacting strategically, one month before judging the market.

8.4 When the launch is quiet (the usual case, which means nothing is broken)

Three diagnostics, in this order:

  1. Are readers finding it? Check the store listing's page views if available (Amazon's Author Central shows per-market data later; store search placement is the main proxy you can check manually). If nobody sees it, fix metadata and ads first — the book isn't the problem.
  2. Who sees it and passes? If views are happening and conversions aren't, the cover, subtitle, or blurb is underselling the book — that's the same conversion work you did in English, with your translator's input.
  3. First reviews: do the newest readers' language reviews come back positive? Those first 10 reviews, in the native language, are worth more than any ad you can buy in month one.

The three levers build on each other in this order: findability → purchase conversion → review velocity. Whatever is broken, it lives in the earliest lever that still functions.

8.5 Pricing mechanics (the current bands)

Repeating it from Chapter 7 (7.7) so it stays near the publish-decision. The 70% vs 35% royalty bands on Amazon are currency-specific and these are guide-points, not current guarantees — check the KDP pricing page at publish time:

  • US: 70% band roughly $2.99–$9.99 (varies slightly with territory pricing).
  • EU stores (Amazon.es/.de/.it/.fr/.co.uk): bands roughly €2.99–€9.99 / £2.99 similar.
  • Japan: ¥75–¥975-ish at 70%, roughly; books under that band are 35% royalty.
  • Brazil/Mexico: currency bands are set periodically; typically BRL 2.99–24.99 / MXN 19.99–99.99-ish at 70% (check at publish time).

When a price sits outside the 70% band, you earn 35% — that's not always wrong (a deliberately cheap $1.99 lead-magnet edition at 35% sometimes outsells 3x its royalty in volume), but a $0.99 translated edition into the German or Japanese store is nearly always under-signaling value. Match-market-value wins.

8.6 Reviews, refunds, and reader communications in-language

  • Read your translated reviews. Pinterest-style tools like Author Central per market, store apps, and the market's own review hubs (Skoob for Brazil, lovelybooks for Germany, Babelio for France) — reviews are overseas readers' conversation and your quality-signal. If you can't read the language, paste into AI and ask for a translation that preserves tone. This is a reading use of AI, and it's one place where AI is honestly excellent.
  • Reply to reader mail in their language. AI-assisted drafting + a reviewer or fluent friend's sanity check handles most reader correspondence, and readers who write in their language and get a reply in that language tell you loudly that you did the work. If volume grows past your ability to respond, hire the social-media-native freelancer from Chapter 7 for 2–4 hours/month of triage.
  • Refunds. Refund rates on translated editions behave like refund rates on English editions: they rise with reviewer-quality problems and price-mismatches. A jump above your normal refund pattern is a bug indicator.

8.7 Maintenance cadence (the "staying live" half)

Translated editions don't die of natural causes; they die of neglect. A light cadence keeps yours healthy without consuming your life:

CadenceWhat to do
Monthly (30 min)Read new reviews (translated via AI), refresh ad bids if advertising, check listing still renders (a store glitch once moved a cover to an auto-detected English one and nobody noticed for months).
Quarterly (2 hours)Price-band and Amazon-policy re-check (KDP terms change; Chapter 02 is the policy-read chapter), promo-calendar pick for next quarter (one local-calendar event per quarter, from Chapter 7's list).
Yearly (one working afternoon)Full re-read of the QA log; consider whether your English source has grown since the freeze; if the English book has a new edition with meaningful content changes (Chapter 4: you froze it for a reason), decide whether to do a second-edition translation pass — which, with your re-reviewer relationship and reusable templates, is dramatically cheaper (~30–50% of the original translation cost, usually a fresh reviewer + targeted review pass).

Thesaurus note on English new-editions: if your English second edition adds a chapter, the translated second edition typically involves AI-drafting that chapter fresh and paying the reviewer per 1,000 words changed (30–50% of full rate). That's the compounding discount of owning the pipeline. You'll never go back to one-off translations once you've done it this way.

8.8 When a market doesn't work (and what to do about it)

Honest worst case: a market just doesn't take, even though the translation is good. Signs: fewer than 25 copies in the first 6 months, no traction from ads, reviews (if any) praise the book and don't drive sales.

What to do, in order:

  1. Check the three levers again (findability → conversion → reviews). 60% of "failed markets" were findability failures with a keyword problem; the book was fine.
  2. Price-drop experiment for 60 days (within reason; in DE/JP, don't undercut the market's floor). A market that won't buy at $5.99 sometimes buys happily at $3.99 where your 35% royalty is still more profit than zero.
  3. Bundle or reposition. In some markets, your book's adjacent topics sell better than its center. A translated "AI for nurses" might underperform, and a translated "AI tools beginners guide" in the same language might fly. This is a metadata and positioning fix, not a new translation.
  4. Go exclusive-or-archive. If nothing moves after two quarters of earnest effort: leave it live (it costs nothing, sells a copy or two per quarter, and builds catalog legibility — the store shelves carry a language's book count into indefiniteness), or unpublish only if you'd be embarrassed for a future foreign-rights deal to coexist with it.

Many authors report that their "flop" translated edition later becomes a warm lead: a foreign-language publisher, agent, or reader arrives because the book exists in their language and asks for a local deal. A live-by-default rule serves that tendency.

Bottom line

Publishing the translated edition is a checklist, not an event. Watch the first 48 hours for rendering and price errors, not for panic-inducing sales numbers. Then run the findability → conversion → reviews ladder, price per market, read your foreign reviews (AI is good at this), and maintain on a light monthly-quarterly-yearly cadence. Translated editions don't die from a bad review; they die from a closed listing, a broken Look-Inside page, a price outside the royalty band, and silence. Boring maintenance is the last skill, and it's what makes translation a compounding asset instead of a one-time cost.
