Rights in Plain English

Chapter 2

The one-sentence version: before translating your book, get a boring, written, definitive answer to "Do I have the exclusive right to publish translations, in this language, through these stores, for these years?" — and if any part of that sentence makes you pause, stop and check before spending money.

This chapter is not legal advice. It is a plain-English map of the rights landscape so you know which questions to ask and which answers should make you suspicious. When real money is on the line, ask a real lawyer.

2.1 The one fact everything rests on

Translations are separate works with their own copyright, and — this is the important part — the right to make them belongs to the owner of the original.

If your book is translated without permission, that translation infringes your copyright. If someone puts a translation of your book on a store, you (or your publisher) can have it removed. Conversely, if you translate your own book, you own that translation yourself — unless your original publishing agreement promises your translations to someone else.

That is the whole foundation. Everything below is about the exceptions that sneak up on people.

2.2 The four situations you might be in

Situation A — You self-published everything, no publisher involved

This is the clean case, and it describes most authors reading a handbook like this one.

You own: the original book, and the right to make and sell translations of it. Translations are new works based on your original, and since you're the original's owner, you can create them, hire someone else to help create them, and sell them.

One wrinkle to know: your translator's work product also matters. If you pay a freelancer to translate (or to review your AI-assisted draft), you want it work-for-hire or a full assignment in writing, so the translation's copyright ends up with you or your LLC, not with the freelancer. This is Chapter 03's paperwork step; keep it in mind here: rights flow in through writing, not through goodwill.

Situation B — You're self-published but under Amazon KDP Select (Kindle Unlimited)

KDP Select does not block translation. KDP Select's exclusivity applies to the digital distribution of the enrolled English edition — it doesn't grant Amazon a lock on your rights to create other editions. Two things to verify though:

  1. The translated edition is a different book file in a different language. You publish it as its own Kindle book, with its own ASIN, in the Spanish or German store. It is not "another distribution of the same title," which would collide with Select. Note: a carousel of translated titles isn't currently a thing in KDP — each language edition is its own book page.
  2. Check the terms when you publish the translation. Amazon's language- and store-specific rules change from time to time. Read the page you're actually publishing through — for five minutes, not five seconds — and if you see language about translations in Select, read that clause specifically.

Also: with a translation of your own book, you own the translation, so you do not need Amazon's or anyone else's permission to publish it — only a clean understanding of where you can distribute it.

Situation C — You have a traditional publisher, or a hybrid press, on the original

This is the situation where people get burned, so slow down.

Standard traditional publishing agreements include a right to create and exploit derivative works, which explicitly includes translations. Depending on how the contract is written, the publisher may:

  • Hold the translation right exclusively — you cannot translate or commission a translation of your own book without the publisher's written permission.
  • Hold it for certain territories or languages — you're free elsewhere.
  • Hold it and be planning to sell foreign rights to a publisher abroad. Many mid-list and indie-press books quietly sell translation rights to foreign publishers for a modest advance. If your publisher does this while you're translating on your own, you can end up with your own unauthorized edition competing with a licensed edition. It would be an innocent, well-meaning mess, and messy is messy.
  • Grant it back after some period — the right reverts to you if they haven't published a translation within X years.

What to do: read your agreement's sections on derivative works, subsidiary rights, foreign rights, and translations. If you can't find the answer in an afternoon of reading, email your editor and ask it in one sentence: "Am I permitted to commission and publish a translation of the book myself, in [language], through [stores], while our agreement is active?" Get the answer in writing, or in a short signed amendment. Do not proceed on a phone-call "sure, no problem."

When a traditional publisher wants to handle translations: that is often a genuinely good deal — they have the infrastructure, editors, contacts, and advance money to do it properly. The trade is that they keep most or all of the translation rights and their revenue. If you're in this situation, weigh a publisher's translation offer against doing it yourself with AI-assisted translation (Chapter 03); for most mid-list books, self-managed translation now grosses the author more, but the publisher route still wins on reach for books with broad international appeal.

Situation D — You wrote with a co-author, ghostwriter, or contributor

If any name other than yours owns a piece of the book, that person owns a piece of the translation right too. Specifically:

  • Co-author with shares: every co-owner must agree to translations and shares in proceeds. If you have a written collaboration agreement, read its "derivative works" line; if you don't have one, make one now that covers translations in plain language ("each co-owner may not commission translations without both signing").
  • Ghostwriter with assignments: if a writing agreement transferred the full copyright to you, translations are yours alone. If the agreement granted you a license rather than full ownership, check whether derivatives are covered.
  • Foreword writers, quoted material, adapted exercises: if you quote someone else's copyrighted text inside your book at any length, the right to translate that quote is theirs, not yours. Newsweek-level short fair-use quotes are usually fine; a quoted paragraph in a foreign language already counts as a new quotation at a (roughly) new scale. For extensive quoted material you don't own, either get permission from the original holder or cut the quote in the translated edition — quietly, and in a way that doesn't change your book's meaning.

2.3 Territory and channels: two words that quietly change what "mine" means

Territory is where a translation can be sold. Channel is through which stores.

Since you own your own rights, the default answer is "everywhere, all stores." Three things to watch:

  1. Amazon's translation program (when open) usually grants Amazon some or all rights to the translation they generate. That's the trade for the free machine-translation-plus-edit service. Read the offer; compare it against "I could pay a human reviewer a few hundred dollars and own everything."
  2. Territory-restricted distributor agreements. If you use a distributor other than a direct store, occasionally agreements include territorial restrictions by default. Unknown territory restrictions are rare but not nonexistent. Read the "Territory" and "License" sections of any publishing platform agreement you sign for a translated edition, the way you would for the original.
  3. Sales-tax and price-consistency realities per country. Chapter 08 walks through why a German market edition priced for Germany is different from one priced for the global store. That's not a rights issue, but it lives next door to the territory conversation.

2.4 Rights paperwork you actually need (short list)

Here is the exact short list. If any item is missing, that's the next thing to fix — not launching faster, not choosing a different cover font.

  1. Proof you're the original's owner. Your KDP account, copyright registration, publisher agreement showing rights granted to you, or — if co-authored — a signed collaboration agreement covering translations.
  2. A written translator/reviewer agreement. One page is enough. It must say: full assignment (or work-for-hire) of copyright in the translation to you, payment terms, confidentiality, no right to publish the translation elsewhere, and a clause saying the deadline and deliverable are concrete. (Chapter 03 has a template.)
  3. A record of the source. Which version of your English file was it frozen at (date + filename)? This prevents "I fixed typos in the original" from quietly making your translated edition out of sync.
  4. Your own tracking of rights. A one-page memo to future-you: "Spanish edition: rights mine, review agreement dated DD/MM/YYYY, exclusivity none, published DD/MM/YYYY, stores XYZ." Fill it per language.
  5. If you'll sell translation rights to another publisher abroad later: a short, separate licensing agreement. You can license "translation and print rights in Germany, 5 years, €X advance" without it affecting your own editions elsewhere. You do not need an agent to make a simple rights-license agreement with a small foreign publisher, but an agent is worth the commission for deals at a major publisher.

2.5 AI-specific rights notes

Because AI is in the mix here, three things come up that didn't use to:

2.5.1 "Who owns AI-assisted translation output?"

Short answer in the practical case: you do, because a human (your reviewer) did the work of taking the AI's raw draft and turning it into publishable, human-quality text. In the United States, the Copyright Office requires human authorship for copyright registration. Raw AI output, unmodified, is a legal gray area; a translation that a fluent human reviewer has substantively reworked, corrected, and approved is a human-authored derivative work built on your original. Work-for-hire language in the reviewer's agreement covers it cleanly in either case.

This is one more reason the human review step is non-negotiable. It is not only for quality. It is what makes the edition a properly owned, human-authored work you can fully control and defend.

2.5.2 "Can a translator claim my translation output belongs to them?"

If your reviewer (or any freelancer) produced a translation under a work-for-hire/assignment agreement, no — they've assigned it to you. If a reviewer produces material outside the agreement (say, an entire re-translation of Chapter 14 on a weekend because they hated your draft), they still own that outside material until you cover it in an amended agreement. Practical rule: amend the agreement briefly whenever a new deliverable appears. One paragraph. Signed. Done.

2.5.3 "Do I have to disclose that AI was used?"

No law requires disclosure in most major markets, and Amazon's KDP terms do not require it for AI-assisted translation where a human has done substantive work. But disclosure is a brand choice, not a requirement. Some authors say clearly on their copyright page: "Spanish edition translated with AI assistance and reviewed by [Name], professional translator." Others don't mention it. The one thing we advise against is claiming a human translator produced 100% of a machine-drafted book. Beyond ethics, it creates a review-time bomb: readers in that language can tell within a page. Be honest and let the quality do the talking.

Platform disclosure policies have been tightening, so re-read the disclosure policy of each store before you publish. Policies have been moving toward "AI-assisted is fine when a human did substantive work; AI-generated with no human step is disallowed" in some categories. Chapter 08 has the pre-publish checklist, which includes a policy check.

2.6 Morality clauses, defamation, and cultural notes (brief but real)

Three non-copyright notes that occasionally bite authors in translation:

  1. Claims that don't travel. A sentence like "the best bookkeeping app in America" is fine in English and weird in Germany, where Amazon has its own top-seller reality. Also, hard medical-claims and promise-of-outcome language often get buffed down by reviewers in healing-adjacent languages. Ask your reviewer to flag any claim that would be legally riskier or factually odd in their language, not just awkward.
  2. Defamation risk of a repeated fact. Facts in your book about specific private individuals generally still need to be accurate in translation, though "translated from the English original" language on your copyright page is a common softener.
  3. Photos and art. If your original book uses photos you licensed from stock or borrowed, check whether the license is worldwide and translation-agnostic (most are). If you license new images for the translated cover (Chapter 06), get worldwide print + digital rights.

2.7 A plain-English rights note you can adapt

Chapter 09 contains a full fill-in template; here's the shape of it in case you want to paste something minimal into your publishing memo today:

Translation rights memo — [Language] edition of [Book Title]

Date: [YYYY-MM-DD]

Original work: [Book Title], copyright (c) [Year] [Author / LLC]. I am the owner of the original work and hold the exclusive right to create and publish translations, per [describe evidence: KDP self-publishing account / signed publisher agreement granting subsidiary rights to me / collaboration agreement dated YYYY-MM-DD signed by all co-owners].

This translation: created by me (as author/LLC) with AI-assisted translation tools, and reviewed, corrected, and approved by [Reviewer Name], a fluent [Language] professional, on [date], under a written agreement dated [date] assigning the translation's copyright to [me/My LLC].

Scope: worldwide, all channels, no exclusivity, no third-party translation license currently in force.

Known limits: [anything relevant — e.g., "quoted excerpt from [Source] used with written permission dated DD/MM/YYYY, permission covers translation into [Language]"; or "none"].

Signed: [Name], [Title], [LLC]

That memo is not a legal document. It is an evidence trail that keeps you honest and keeps any future co-publisher, accountant, translator, or lawyer from having to reconstruct your rights from email threads.

Bottom line

You almost certainly have the right to translate your own book — if you self-published and nothing in any agreement you signed says otherwise. The three checks before money moves: (1) find the derivative-works / translation clause in any publishing or collaboration agreement that touches your book; (2) get a written assignment from your translator or reviewer; (3) when a "free translation" platform (like Amazon's translation program) offers to do it for you, read exactly which rights you're granting them in exchange. AI doesn't change any of this — it just makes the "do it yourself and keep everything" path affordable for ordinary authors, which is exactly why we like it.
